Pocket break buzzer

Product design, AVR firmware, 3D-printed enclosure — prototype

Portfolio CV (English) Lebenslauf (DE)

Overview

Call-center and customer-service agents break at the same fixed times. A phone is the obvious timer, and the obvious distraction. This is a small battery-powered device with one button: press it, and it buzzes when the break is over. No screen, no app, no phone.

Where it stands: prototype, in progress. The design is finished (v3 shells), the STL files are with the print shop for the first print, and the electronics are being assembled next. Nothing is tested in daily use yet and nothing is for sale. Everything below is the design as built, not a shipped product.

The problem it solves

Breaks run on a fixed clock and everyone's break starts at the same minute. The two things people actually need to know are when the break ends and when to head back. A phone answers both, at the cost of picking up the phone during the one part of the shift that is supposed to be a rest. A watch works, but nobody wants to reset a countdown four times a shift.

The device is deliberately dumb: one button, one vibration motor, four gestures. It has no radio, no screen and no way to distract anyone.

How it is used

GestureWhat it does
Double pressStart a 15-minute break.
Hold 1 secondStart a 45-minute lunch.
Single pressStop and cancel the running timer.
Hold 4 secondsCycle the early warning: off, 3 minutes before the end (one buzz), 8 minutes before the end (two buzzes).

Electronics

  • Controller: ATtiny13A-PU, plain C with avr-gcc, no Arduino core. The firmware fits in 994 of the 1024 bytes of flash. The chosen early-warning setting is written to EEPROM, so it survives a battery change.
  • Input: one 12 x 12 x 4.3 mm tactile switch on PB3, read with the internal pull-up.
  • Output: an 8 mm coin vibration motor on PB0, driven through a BC337 transistor with a 1 kΩ base resistor and a 1N4148 flyback diode across the motor.
  • Power: two AAA cells. The case is the battery holder: spring contact plates at each end, with about 30 mm of slack in the series wire so the cells can be lifted out.
  • Build: through-hole on 7 x 9 cm vero board, chip in an 8-pin socket, flashed with a USBasp.

No LED and no display, on purpose. The only thing a person needs to know is whether the break is over, and a buzz says that in the pocket without a light that gives the device away in a dim room.

The case

Two shells, 50 x 64 x 17 mm, press fit, with the button at one end and the motor at the other so the buzz lands away from the thumb.

The seam is designed for a printer, not for a machinist: a quarter millimetre of clearance so the two shells still fit after the printer's own tolerance, 0.7 mm bumps that hold the press fit, and a thumbnail notch at the back to open it.

The model is parametric. Every dimension is a variable in a Python script that builds the two shells with mesh booleans, so changing the switch height or the motor size is one number and a rebuild, not a re-draw. Each iteration is then checked by measurement rather than by eye: interference between the shells and the real switch, cell, contact plate and compressed spring; shell-to-shell overlap; and the down-facing area that decides whether the print needs supports.

Iterations: what the checks caught

v1 — first layoutTwo shells with the button on the long edge. Printed as a preview render only; the layout put the thumb across the seam and left no room for the cell contacts.
v2 — opposite endsButton and motor moved to opposite ends, dish centred on the button. A cross-section check found a real fault: the battery contact plate pockets were cut 1.8 mm deep for plates that are 0.3 mm thick, so the plates would have sat in a trench and the cells would have rattled instead of being held.
v3 — the version being printedPockets cut to 0.30 mm with a shallow relief slot behind each plate, because the plate pin spacing is not published. The pedestals the print shop flagged as loose were filled into the arch. The thumb dish was deepened to a 0.69 mm concentric depression. Verified by interference checks on the plate, cell, spring and shell overlap, and by a down-facing area check on the print orientation.
Why no supports in the dish. Printed face down, the dish is not a bridge: it is a 0.69 mm void between the part and the build plate, three or four layers at 0.2 mm. The first layers simply land on the bed, and the opening closes from 19 mm down to the 7.8 mm button hole over four layers. The dish will come out slightly shallower than modelled and a little rough inside, which does not matter for a thumb guide. The print instruction is supports off, 0.2 mm layers.

Parts and printing

The electronics come from a component shop in Cairo, ordered as a single-unit bill of materials: ATtiny13A-PU, 8-pin socket, BC337, 1N4148, 1 kΩ resistor, RCD0820 8 mm coin motor, AAA contact plate set, two AAA cells and a piece of vero board. Nine lines, about 180 EGP at single-unit prices; the switch and the programmer were already in my kit.

The shells are printed in PLA by a local print shop in Cairo, CreatoTech, at 0.2 mm layers with no supports. The first pair is on order from the v3 files; before that, the same shop printed the earlier pair and flagged the loose pedestals that v3 fixed.

What is next

  • Assemble the first unit and flash the firmware.
  • Calibrate the 15 and 45 minute timers against a stopwatch: the ATtiny13A runs on its internal RC oscillator, which is only good to about ±10%, so the firmware constants may need trimming.
  • Carry it for a week of real shifts before deciding whether it is worth building more than one.

Tools and skills shown

AVR C firmware (avr-gcc, EEPROM storage, debounce and gesture timing), low-side transistor drive with flyback protection, parametric CAD in Python (trimesh booleans), FDM print orientation and support decisions, tolerance and interference checking, single-unit BOM and sourcing.
